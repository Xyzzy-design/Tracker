Warm-up and preparation

Practical routine
  1. Start with 5–8 minutes of easy cycling or walking.
  2. Before your first heavy lift, do 2–3 progressively heavier warm-up sets. Example: 10 easy reps, 5 moderate reps, then 2–3 reps near working weight. Do not exhaust yourself.
  3. For later exercises, add a lighter rehearsal set when needed. Warm-up sets do not count as working sets.

Strength and muscle growth

Evidence [1]

Use heavier loads for strength and a variety of challenging loads for muscle growth. Put your priority lift early in the session.

  • Strength emphasis: roughly 4–8 reps on major lifts; heavier loads around 80% of maximum or above are especially useful for maximal strength.
  • Growth emphasis: 6–15 reps is a convenient default; 12–20 works well for many isolation exercises. Other ranges can also build muscle.
  • Machines and dumbbells are both valid. Choose movements you can perform consistently and comfortably.

Progressive overload and RIR

Evidence [1] + practical method

RIR means repetitions in reserve: how many additional clean repetitions you estimate you could complete. Usually stop with 1–3 left; use 2–3 when learning or fatigued. Failure is not required.

Double progression example: bench press, 3 × 8–12

40 lb per hand: 10/9/8 → 11/10/9 → 12/12/12. Once all sets reach 12 with good technique and 1–2 RIR, try the next small dumbbell increment. Expect repetitions to fall toward 8 again.

If you cannot reach the lower end with clean form, reduce the load. Compare the same exercise, setup, range and rest intervals. Do not force increases every session.

Volume, frequency and your split

Evidence [1]

About 10 challenging weekly sets per muscle group is a useful growth target, not a mandatory minimum. Smaller doses can work; add volume only when recovery and performance support it. Training a muscle twice weekly is a convenient way to distribute work.

Your Tuesday and Sunday sessions both train quads and chest; Friday and Sunday train back. Hamstrings receive work Tuesday and Friday. Count direct sets first and recognize that pressing and pulling also train assisting muscles; do not count every secondary muscle as a full direct set.

Keep your current plan stable for several weeks before making changes. If a priority muscle stalls and recovery is good, consider adding 1–2 weekly sets.

Rest between sets

Evidence [2] + practical defaults
  • Heavy leg press, bench press, rows, RDL and squats: 2–3 minutes; longer if necessary to restore performance.
  • Curls, flyes, raises, calf work and crunches: 60–120 seconds; extend if repetitions drop sharply.
  • Do not rush to maximize sweating. Rest should support quality work. Research suggests a modest advantage over very short rests, but no clear universal optimum beyond 90 seconds for growth.

Technique and safety

Practical coaching cues
  • Use a comfortable, controlled range. Lower smoothly, avoid bouncing, and lift without unnecessary momentum. No mandatory slow tempo.
  • Brace your trunk before loaded movements; breathe between reps and avoid prolonged breath holding.
  • Leg press: keep hips against pad. Goblet squat: keep whole foot grounded. RDL: push hips back, keep weights close and stop before losing spinal control.
  • Bench and pec deck: keep shoulders stable, avoid painful depth. Rows: avoid jerking. Pulldown: pull toward upper chest rather than behind the neck.
  • Calf raises: use stable support and controlled ankle movement. Captain’s chair knee raise: curl the pelvis upward and avoid swinging.
  • Stop for sharp pain, dizziness or unusual symptoms. Seek urgent care for chest pain, fainting or severe breathlessness.

Nutrition and supplements

Evidence [3–4]

At 72 kg, a practical protein goal of 1.6–2.0 g/kg is about 115–144 g daily. Distribute it across 3–5 meals. Eat enough total energy to support training; a modest surplus can help if muscle gain is the priority.

  • Creatine monohydrate: 3–5 g daily, including rest days; loading is optional. Ask a clinician first if you have kidney disease or relevant medical concerns.
  • CoQ10 and B-complex are not established requirements for muscle growth. Review doses, medication interactions and whether supplementation is needed with a clinician or pharmacist.
  • Drink water regularly and eat a balanced diet; supplements do not replace food or sleep.

Recovery and deloading

Evidence [5] + practical method

Aim for 7–9 hours of sleep and use nonlifting days for easy activity. Adults generally need at least 7 hours regularly.

If performance declines across several sessions, soreness persists or motivation and sleep worsen, consider a lighter week: reduce working sets by roughly one-third to one-half, keep loads comfortable and leave extra RIR. This is a flexible coaching strategy, not a required schedule.

Track performance trends over weeks rather than judging one bad workout. Persistent pain or fatigue warrants professional evaluation.

Common training myths

Evidence [1]
  • No soreness, no growth: soreness is not a reliable measure of muscle gain.
  • Every set must reach failure: challenging nonfailure sets work.
  • Only barbells build strength: machines and dumbbells can be effective.
  • One perfect rep range: multiple loading ranges support growth.
  • Change exercises every week: consistency makes progress easier to measure.

Cardio and recovery

Aim for 150 minutes of moderate or 75 minutes of vigorous aerobic activity per week, or an equivalent combination. Build gradually. Moderate effort generally allows talking but not singing. Keep demanding cardio away from heavy leg sessions if it compromises recovery; prioritize strength first when both are performed together and strength is the main goal.

Compare pace and heart rate under similar conditions; treadmill incline is percent grade, not degrees. Machine calorie estimates are approximate.
